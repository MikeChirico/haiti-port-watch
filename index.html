Haiti port monitoring · 2021–2026

Counting Haiti’s ships from space

No public record tracks the cargo ships arriving in Haiti. Counting them in free satellite images, every clear day for five years, shows Port-au-Prince now holds twice as many ships on a typical day as it did two years ago.

Haiti port-traffic project · Data through 15 October 2026

Haiti imports about 80% of its food. Rice, flour, cooking oil, cement and medicine come in on cargo ships at a handful of ports, above all Port-au-Prince. When shipping is disrupted, shortages and price rises follow.

That makes a simple question very important: how many ships are in Haiti’s ports right now, and is that number going up or down? Aid agencies planning food deliveries, economists watching prices and donors deciding where to send support all need the answer.

Until now there has been no reliable way to get it.

The blind spot

Why nobody could see these ships

In most countries, ship traffic is easy to follow. Vessels broadcast their position, port authorities publish arrival statistics, and analysts buy the data. In Haiti, each of these sources breaks down.

Not available

Ship tracking (AIS)

Large ships broadcast their identity and position by radio. But the data is sold commercially, coverage depends on receivers near the coast, transponders can be switched off, and the small coastal freighters common in Haiti are not required to carry one.

This project has no AIS data, and none is expected.
Not available

Port and customs records

There is no consistent, timely public series of ship arrivals. Port operations have also been repeatedly disrupted by insecurity, which is exactly when figures are needed most.

No consistent public series to build on.
Not safe

People on the ground

Sending observers to watch the harbour every day is costly and, in parts of Port-au-Prince, dangerous. It also cannot be repeated back in time.

No historical record.
Tried and dropped

Radar satellites

Sentinel-1 radar sees through cloud, so we tried it first. Its counts were dominated by false alarms from piers, buoys and shallow reefs, and they did not track real ship numbers.

Correlation with real ships: r = 0.055. About 5× overcount.
What worked

Optical satellites

Free Sentinel-2 imagery shows ships as small bright hulls on dark water. With the right detectors, every clear image becomes a count, going back years.

Agrees with human reviewers: r = 0.95.

The eye in the sky

A camera that passes every few days

The European Union’s Sentinel-2 satellites orbit 786 km up and photograph every coastline on Earth. Three of them now share the work, so each Haitian port is imaged every two to five days, always in the late morning local time. Each pixel covers 10 × 10 metres. The images are free to anyone.

The catch is weather. A cloud over the harbour hides everything beneath it, so the first step is to throw out every image where the port is not at least 85% clear.

What a ship looks like from orbit

This is the anchorage off Cap-Haïtien, Haiti’s second port, on 10 October 2023. Ships waiting for a berth sit in a loose line offshore. Can you spot them?

A detector built by the Allen Institute for AI flagged seven possible ships here. A reviewer checked each one and confirmed five, boxed here. The detector was trained on about 37,000 ships labelled by experts around the world, and a second AI model estimates each vessel’s length and type.

Up close, a large cargo ship is only a short streak of about 17 pixels. That is enough to tell a ship from open water, but it is near the limit of what 10-metre imagery can show.

The hard case

Ships at the pier hide in plain sight

A ship tied up against a concrete quay blends into it at 10-metre resolution. The global detector almost never finds them. So we built a second method that asks a different question: is something sitting on water that is normally open?

For every pixel along the piers, the method checks how often it looked like water across all other clear images in the five-year record. When a usually-open pixel suddenly isn’t water, something is probably there. A trained filter then checks each candidate against 25 measurements, such as its shape, its contrast with the sea and how often that spot has flipped before. The filter removes most false alarms from shadows, wet concrete and construction.

Checking the machine

Checked against a human reviewer

An automated count is only useful if people can trust it. A reviewer went through a sample of 60 satellite passes across all three ports from 2023 to 2026: the busiest days, the quietest days and a random set. They confirmed or rejected every detection and drew a box around every ship the detectors missed. Four passes were too hazy to judge, which left 56.

Overall the counts run slightly low, at 0.92 times the reviewer’s total, and the bias differs by port. Port-au-Prince reads about 25% low, because it has many small boats and berthed ships the detectors miss. Cap-Haïtien reads about 25% high, and Port Lafito, which has very few ships, about 50% high. For that reason the trends are more reliable than the exact levels.

What the reviewers caught

The ship that never moved

Real ships come and go. Even a ship waiting at anchor swings with the wind and eventually leaves. So the latest update to the model asks a new question of every detection: does it ever move?

All counted detections from five years are grouped by location. A spot is masked out of the counts if something sits within 15 metres of the same point on at least 40% of clear days for over a year, or if reviewers keep rejecting it and have found almost no real ships there.

One spot met both tests. On a shoal just west of the main terminal in Port-au-Prince, the detector found a “139 m cargo ship” on 171 of 244 clear days, from 2021 to 2026. Reviewers checked it 12 times and rejected it every time. It is almost certainly a wreck.

The same 500 × 500 m patch, one clear December–March day from each year. The object never moves, but the detector counted it as a large ship each time.

Masking it removes about 0.7 from Port-au-Prince’s daily count in every year. The trend is unchanged, but the levels are now more honest. On the reviewed passes the mask removed 11 detections, all of them rejected by reviewers, and no real ships.

The rule is deliberately cautious. Busy berths also show detections in the same place, but they are real ships. Port Lafito’s quay, for example, was rejected 8 times by reviewers but also held 3 real ships, so it is flagged for the pier filter rather than masked.

What five years show

Port-au-Prince is busier than at any point since 2021

With every clear image counted from October 2021 to October 2026, a clear picture emerges. Port-au-Prince held steady at about two vessels on a typical clear day until 2024. Then it climbed, to 4.2 vessels per clear day in 2025–26, more than double the 2.0 of 2023–24, peaking in May 2026. Almost all of the increase is ships waiting at anchor, not at the piers. Vessels 60 m or longer rose from 1.3 to 3.1 per day.

Cap-Haïtien moved the other way, from 2.8 to 1.6 vessels per clear day, as its once-crowded anchorage emptied. Port Lafito stayed quiet throughout.

Reading the numbers

What this data can and can’t tell you

Snapshots, not port callsEach count is the number of vessels present at about 10:25 local time on a clear day. A ship that waits a week is counted on every pass. Counts measure presence and congestion, not tonnes of cargo.
Clear days onlyCloudy days have no count. Each port gets roughly one clear look a week, fewer in the storm season.
Trends over exact levelsAccuracy varies by port, and the human review covers 2023–2026. Changes over time are the most reliable signal.
Fixed objects are maskedSpots where a “ship” never moves are removed from the counts. A new fixed object is caught once it has recurred for about a year, or sooner if reviewers flag it.
Sizes and types are estimatesLength and vessel type come from an AI model. They are useful guides, but they can be wrong on faint or docked ships.

What was delivered

An open, repeatable record

The project produced daily and monthly vessel counts for Port-au-Prince, Cap-Haïtien and Port Lafito from October 2021 to October 2026. Each count is split into ships at anchor and at the pier, ships of 60 m or longer, and cargo or tanker types. It is built entirely from free satellite imagery and openly published AI models, with no commercial data, so it can be extended as each new image arrives.

For the first time, anyone working on Haiti can see whether ships are arriving, waiting or staying away, without depending on data that does not exist.

Beyond Haiti

The same approach works wherever shipping matters and data is missing

Nothing in this method is specific to Haiti. Sentinel-2 photographs every coastline on Earth every few days, the images are free, and the detectors are openly published. The approach fits anywhere ship traffic matters but cannot be tracked by the usual means: where AIS is absent or switched off, where port statistics are not published, or where it is not safe to watch the harbour in person.

Setting up a new port takes three steps: draw the area to count, run the detectors over the image archive, and have a person review a sample of passes to measure accuracy. The main limit is weather. Ports under frequent cloud get fewer clear looks.

Built for this project

A review tool for checking satellite detections

Off-the-shelf labelling tools are built for photos, not for scrolling through hundreds of satellite passes of the same harbour. So the project built its own. It became the backbone of both the accuracy checks and the training of the pier filter.

  • vOne keystroke per decision. Mark a detection valid, invalid or skip, and move on.
  • aScene checks. Draw a box around every ship the detectors missed, so the tool measures what was missed as well as what was found.
  • bSeveral views. Switch between true colour, infrared and a water index, or flick to the same spot on another date to see whether a “ship” is really a fixed structure.
  • ↧Exports for analysis. Labels save as map files and as training sets for new detectors, with a per-port dashboard of accuracy.

Used to review the 60-pass validation sample and to label 188 pier detections for training the filter. It has since grown into a desktop app with projects, guided setup and model-assisted labelling.
